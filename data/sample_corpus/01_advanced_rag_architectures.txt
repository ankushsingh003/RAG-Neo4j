# Advanced Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Architecture Specification

## 1. Limitations of Standard Naive RAG
Naive RAG relies strictly on dense vector embeddings and top-k approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search using cosine distance or dot product. While effective for thematic semantic matching, standard vector search suffers from critical real-world failure modes:
1. Keyword and Term Mismatch: Dense vectors frequently overlook exact technical identifiers, part numbers, version strings, or domain-specific acronyms such as "CVE-2024-38077" or "ISO-27001-Clause-9.2".
2. Out-of-Domain Embedding Degradation: When domain vocabulary diverges from pretraining distributions, semantic similarity degenerates into noisy matches.
3. Lack of Structural Understanding: Dense embeddings compress paragraph meaning into a single dense vector (typically 384 or 1536 dimensions), losing multi-hop relationships between distant entities.

## 2. Hybrid Retrieval with BM25 Okapi and Dense Vector Embeddings
To bridge lexical precision and semantic comprehension, modern enterprise retrieval employs Hybrid Search.
- Sparse Lexical Search: BM25 (Best Matching 25) calculates document relevance based on Term Frequency (TF), Inverse Document Frequency (IDF), and document length normalization. Parameters include k1 (typically 1.2 to 1.5 for term frequency saturation) and b (typically 0.75 for length penalty). BM25 guarantees that rare exact terms receive heavy weight.
- Dense Vector Search: Dense retrievers use bi-encoder neural networks such as `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` or `BGE-large-en-v1.5` to embed user queries and document chunks into shared metric spaces. Cosine similarity captures semantic paraphrasing and synonyms.

## 3. Candidate Fusion Strategies: RRF vs Convex Linear Combination
When merging ranked lists from sparse (BM25) and dense retrievers, two primary fusion algorithms are deployed:
1. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF):
   RRF calculates a combined score for each document d across retrieval methods M according to the formula:
   RRF_Score(d) = SUM_{m in M} [ 1 / (k + rank_m(d)) ]
   Here, k is a smoothing constant, typically set to 60. RRF is scale-invariant and does not require calibrating heterogeneous raw score distributions between BM25 scores (unbounded positive floats) and cosine similarities ([-1, 1]).
2. Convex Linear Combination (Weighted Score Fusion):
   Linear fusion normalizes both sparse scores and dense scores to a uniform [0, 1] range using MinMax or Sigmoid normalization:
   Score_Hybrid(d) = alpha * Score_Dense_norm(d) + (1 - alpha) * Score_BM25_norm(d)
   The parameter alpha balances lexical and semantic priority (alpha = 0.5 represents an equal split).

## 4. Cross-Encoder Reranking
While bi-encoders produce separate embeddings for query and document, a Cross-Encoder passes the query and candidate passage simultaneously through cross-attention layers (e.g., `cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2` or `bge-reranker-large`).
Cross-attention calculates token-to-token interactions between every word of the query and every word of the passage, eliminating the information bottleneck of single vector pooling.
In a production pipeline, Hybrid retrieval gathers top-25 candidate passages (high recall phase), and the Cross-Encoder re-ranks these into the top-3 to top-5 most relevant passages (high precision phase) before feeding them to the generator LLM.
